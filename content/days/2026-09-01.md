## The thread

Ruth 4 and 2 John are both about a door, and they swing opposite ways.

At the Bethlehem gate a Moabite woman is taken into an Israelite family in front of ten witnesses, in the face of a law saying that no Moabite shall enter the assembly of the LORD "even to the tenth generation" (Deut 23:3) - and the genealogy closing the book runs exactly ten names from Perez to David. The elder in 2 John tells a congregation the reverse: if a teacher arrives without the confession that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh, do not receive him into the house, and do not even greet him (vv10-11). In a community that had already lost people who "went out from us" (1 John 2:19), that door is being shut on someone who used to be inside it.

What makes these the same argument rather than opposite ones is what each door turns on. Ruth is received without ceasing to be a Moabite - the narrator keeps the label on her until the moment Boaz names her as his wife - because of what she had already declared: "your people shall be my people, and your God my God" (1:16). The travelling teacher is refused despite every credential of insider standing, because of what he says about Christ. Neither door is worked by ancestry, respectability or reputation. Both hang on allegiance.

Isaiah 35 lays the same criterion out along a road. The Way of Holiness carries a purity rule, "the unclean shall not pass over it" (35:8), and then describes the people actually walking it in the only terms it offers: they are **the redeemed**, the **ransomed of the LORD**, coming to Zion with singing (35:9-10). Nothing else is said about who they are. They are qualified by having been bought.

Which puts Boaz's word back in play. The *go'el* running through Ruth 4 - the kinsman who takes on a family's debt at cost to his own estate, where the nearer relative had done the arithmetic and declined - is the same idea Isaiah reaches for in calling the returning exiles redeemed. Naomi's neighbours bless God for not leaving her "without a redeemer" (4:14) and mean a newborn baby; Isaiah means a road out of exile; the elder writes to people who were bought by a Christ who really came in flesh, which is precisely the point he will not let go. In each case someone else absorbs the cost first, and belonging follows from that rather than from anything the included party brought with them.
