# Judges 15 - Background & Links

**Passage snapshot**  
Samson returns for the wife he walked out on, finds her given to his best man, and answers with a spiral of reprisal: burnt harvests, a slaughter at Etam, and a thousand men killed with a donkey's jawbone after his own tribe binds him and hands him over.

## Map of the text
- 15:1-3: At the wheat harvest Samson comes with a young goat; the father has given his wife away and offers the younger sister; Samson declares himself blameless in what follows.
- 15:4-5: Three hundred *shu'alim* caught, tied tail to tail with torches, loosed into standing grain, sheaves, vineyards and olive groves.
- 15:6-8: The Philistines burn the woman and her father; Samson strikes them "hip and thigh" and withdraws to the cleft of the rock of Etam.
- 15:9-13: Three thousand men of Judah go down to bind him, protesting that the Philistines rule over them; Samson agrees to be handed over.
- 15:14-17: The Spirit rushes on him, the ropes melt like burnt flax, and he kills a thousand with a fresh donkey's jawbone; the taunt-song plays on *hamor* (donkey) and *homer* (heap).
- 15:18-20: Desperate thirst; his first recorded prayer; God splits open the hollow at Lehi; the spring is named En-hakkore. He judges Israel twenty years.

## Historical-cultural background (what an ancient Israelite would notice)
- The timing in 15:1 is not decoration. Standing grain at wheat harvest, plus vineyards and olive groves, is the entire agricultural year in one strike. Olive trees take decades to replace. This is calculated economic ruin, and the Philistine reprisal against the woman is the predictable answer.
- The bride-price customs behind 15:1-2 matter. Samson left after the wedding feast without consummating the marriage, so from the father's side the union had lapsed and giving her to the best man was defensible. Samson's "this time I shall be blameless" claims the moral high ground the narrator never grants him.
- *Shu'alim* covers both foxes and jackals, and jackals fit better - they are pack animals and could be trapped in numbers, where foxes are solitary. Translators have gone both ways.
- The fresh jawbone is another corpse: Numbers 6:6 forbids a Nazirite to go near a dead body, and Samson has now defiled himself by lion, by feast, and by weapon. The vow is being spent piece by piece.
- The most telling detail is Judah's three thousand. A tribe fields an army - against its own deliverer, on behalf of the occupier, saying plainly "the Philistines rule over us" (15:11). Israel has stopped experiencing Philistine rule as a problem.

## Audience needs (why this mattered to Israel)
- The judge cycle of Judges 2:16-19 assumes Israel cries out and God raises a deliverer. Here nobody cries out. Samson acts from private grievance, and Israel actively resists being delivered.
- An audience asking why the land was never fully taken is being shown the answer at close range: accommodation. Judah is not oppressed into silence, it is comfortable enough to police its own.
- Verse 18 is the hinge the audience is meant to feel. After a thousand men, Samson nearly dies of thirst and finally speaks to God. Strength was never the thing he lacked.

## Canonical placement
- Late pre-monarchic period, in the Philistine ascendancy of the Iron Age, with Judah under settled foreign control rather than episodic raiding.
- The chapter deepens the book's argument for kingship. A deliverer without covenant loyalty, and a people without appetite for deliverance, cannot produce rest, and the twenty-year notice in 15:20 is a low ceiling compared with the forty and eighty years of earlier judges.
- Samson's spring at Lehi echoes the wilderness water miracles, which places even this episode inside God's provision, but the resemblance is uncomfortable: Israel in the wilderness quarrelled with God, and Samson here quarrels on his own behalf.

## Scripture links
- Nazirite corpse prohibition: Num 6:6, against the fresh jawbone of 15:15.
- Improvised weapons against Philistines: Judg 3:31, Shamgar and the oxgoad.
- The cycle the chapter breaks: Judg 2:16-19; contrast the crying out of Judg 3:9, 3:15, 4:3, 6:6.
- Water from the rock: Exod 17:1-7 and Num 20:2-13, both named for the complaint that prompted them, as En-hakkore is named for a cry.
- Vengeance reserved to God: Deut 32:35, which Samson's "let me be avenged" (15:7, 16:28) never quite honours.
- Judah handing over its deliverer to the occupying power is a pattern the canon returns to, though the connection is thematic rather than cited.

## Message to the first hearers
- A people can be so long under a foreign power that they mistake the arrangement for peace, and defend it against the one disturbing it.
- Private vengeance and public deliverance can look identical from outside and be nothing alike underneath. God uses Samson without endorsing him.
- Consecration is not spent all at once. Lion, feast, jawbone: each step is small, each is a breach, and the hair is all that is left.
- The first honest word Samson speaks to God comes when his strength has run out, which is the chapter's quiet verdict on his strength.
