# Isaiah 25 - Background & Links

**Passage snapshot**  
The answer to chapter 24. The earth has been emptied and the LORD has taken his throne on Zion; now a thanksgiving hymn breaks out, and at its centre stands the most astonishing promise in the book so far: a feast on that mountain for all peoples, and death itself swallowed up.

## Map of the text
- 25:1-5: Praise for plans "formed of old"; a fortified city made a heap, the palace of foreigners no longer a city; ruthless nations forced to honour him; the LORD a refuge to the poor and needy, shade in heat, shelter in storm.
- 25:6-8: On this mountain, a feast of rich food and well-aged wine for all peoples; the covering over all peoples swallowed up; death swallowed up forever; tears wiped from all faces; the reproach of his people taken away from all the earth.
- 25:9: The response - "Behold, this is our God; we have waited for him."
- 25:10-12: A jarring coda. The LORD's hand rests on this mountain, but Moab is trodden down like straw in a dung pit, its pride laid low.

## Historical-cultural background (what an ancient Israelite would notice)
- "This mountain" carries straight over from 24:23, where the LORD reigns on Mount Zion. The feast is not somewhere new; it is spread on the ground the previous chapter cleared.
- Ancient Near Eastern kings held accession banquets, and a great feast was the ordinary way a new reign was inaugurated. Israel had its own version at Sinai, where the elders "beheld God, and ate and drank" (Exod 24:9-11). A hearer would recognise this as an enthronement meal.
- What is not ordinary is the guest list. The feast is for *all peoples*, and it is set on Zion. Nations appear in the prophets far more often under judgement than at table.
- "Swallow up death forever" lands hard in a world where Canaanite myth cast Mot, death, as the great swallower. The verb is turned around: the swallower is swallowed.
- The identity of the ruined city in 25:2 is genuinely disputed. Babylon, Moab's capital, and a deliberately unnamed type of every arrogant city have all been argued, and the text supplies no name.
- The "covering" and "veil" of 25:7 are likewise contested: a mourning shroud, or a blindness over the nations. Both readings have support, and the funeral-shroud sense fits the death imagery that follows.

## Audience needs (why this mattered to Judah)
- A small people watching imperial power do as it pleased needed to hear that the ruthless are not permanent and that God is shelter for the poor and needy specifically (25:4).
- Grief was the immediate reality: siege, deportation, bereavement. The promise is not that grief was unreal but that God will personally wipe the tears away.
- "The reproach of his people he will take away from all the earth" (25:8) speaks to a nation whose humiliation was public. Their standing before the nations would be reversed by God, not by arms.
- The Moab coda keeps the comfort from turning into complacency. The mountain is a place of refuge and of judgement in the same breath, and Judah is not exempt from the standard being applied.

## Canonical placement
- The second movement of the "Isaiah Apocalypse" (chs 24-27), where judgement on the whole earth resolves into worldwide salvation centred on Zion.
- It reaches back to Sinai's covenant meal and forward to the messianic banquet, making the table the recurring image of covenant fellowship restored.
- 25:8 is the Old Testament's clearest statement that death is not final, and the New Testament treats it as the promise the resurrection fulfils.

## Scripture links
- Covenant meal on the mountain: Exod 24:9-11.
- Immediate context: Isa 24:23, the LORD reigning on Zion before his elders.
- Free invitation to a feast: Isa 55:1-2.
- Death swallowed up: quoted at 1 Cor 15:54 as the word fulfilled in resurrection; cf. Hos 13:14.
- Tears wiped away: Rev 7:17 and Rev 21:4, which also drops "death shall be no more".
- The banquet for all peoples: Matt 8:11 (many from east and west reclining with Abraham), Luke 14:15-24, and the marriage supper of Rev 19:9.
- Moab's pride brought low: Isa 16:6-14, the earlier oracle this coda recalls.

## Message to the first hearers
- The God who empties the earth in chapter 24 sets a table in chapter 25. Judgement is not the last word, and it was never the point.
- The feast is for all peoples, which means Judah's God was never merely Judah's, and the nations are destined for the table rather than only the sword.
- Death is named as an enemy God will destroy, not a boundary he merely works around. For a people well acquainted with burial, that is the promise underneath all the others.
- Waiting is vindicated. "We have waited for him" is said on the far side, by people who had no evidence at the time that waiting was worth it.
